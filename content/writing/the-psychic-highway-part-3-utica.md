***The Formula***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1ddc00b1-eaa5-492a-992c-6aa9fb9af1e9_1824x914.png)

*A political cartoon depicting the violence and intimidation that defined the “Mohawk Valley Formula.” Credit: James Lerner*

IN 1936, JAMES RAND JR. released the “Mohawk Valley Formula,” his personal salve for labor agitations that were crescendoing as the New Deal new-ly empowered workers across the country. At the time, Rand Jr. was the president of the world's leading manufacturer of office equipment and typewriters, the Central New York-based Remington Rand corporation.

Just two years earlier, the workers of Remington Rand had triumphed against Rand Jr. in a battle for unionization, formally gathering with workers of the Underwood Typewriter Company under the banner of the *District Council of Office Equipment Workers*. Rand Jr., displeased with such an imposition of democratic order, devoted himself to flagrantly violating the Union’s contract at every possible turn.

When 6,500 union workers walked away from his Mohawk Valley plants in 1936 in protest, he responded with a strategy that was every bit as bitter and vindictive as it was practical. Published as a pamphlet by the National Association of Manufacturers (NAM), he prescribed a nine-step plan of action, reproduced in short here and deployed to great effect against the Remington Rand strikers:

*Label union leaders as “agitators” to discredit them with the public.*

*Raise high the banner of “law and order,” causing the community to forget that employees have equal rights with other community members.*

*Call a “mass meeting” to coordinate public sentiment against the strike; develop and strengthen a “Citizens’ Committee.”*

*Form a large police force to intimidate the strikers.*

*Convince the strikers that their cause is hopeless with a “back-to-work” movement; hire a puppet force of “loyal employees.”*

*Set a date for re-opening the plant and frame it as a final plea of the “loyal employees.”*

*Stage the re-opening theatrically by marching employees in with the protection of armed police.*

*Demoralize the strikers with a continuing show of force, if necessary turning the locality into a barricaded, warlike camp.*

*Conclude by reporting that the plant is in full operation and the strikers are attempting to interfere with the “right to work”.*

The National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), established in 1935 to provide federal oversight for labor negotiations, called the Mohawk Valley Formula a “battle plan for industrial war” in a scathing, 120-page review of Remington Rand’s behavior during the strike. Rand Jr. won the fight for his factory — the plant was back up and operating with at least 4,000 new employees the next year — but he lost the battle for public support. His antics, which had included falsely proclaiming plant closures, hiring dozens of security guards to harass and beat up strikers, and employing fake workers to “return to work,” soured the local population on Remington Rand’s management.

These tactics also drew the attention of the national media: in one particularly bizarre incident from 1937, Secretary of Labor Frances Perkins directly sought out Rand Jr. to mediate the strike, only to find that his administrative staff “did not know where he was” and “did not know how to contact him.” Rand Jr. only responded to Perkins when she took out a full-page open letter in major national newspapers, and even then he would not discuss the strike with her.

It took until 1940, after a circus of court cases, appeals, convictions, and acquittals, for Remington Rand to comply with the NLRB’s ruling and reinstate the striking workers. It’s hard to say if this was all worth it for Remington Rand, which was ensnared in the mid-century corporate meat grinder and emerged as part of a global IT consulting company.

But for Rand Jr., it was a cause worth dying for. He believed himself to be a defender of a free market system that was under attack by the New Deal, his dealings in Central New York mere skirmishes on a crusade for the spirit of America itself. And he certainly had a claim to representing the WASPish colonial sentiments of that imagined America. In many ways, not the least of which was his New England background, Rand Jr. was the archetypal depression-era American businessman: he was a Harvard-educated old-stock Puritan whose family had migrated around the original colonies in search of enterprise.

They had found their future in the corporate office, where millions of businessmen would soon start churning through the typewriters and papers that were tabulating explosive industrial profits. It’s fitting, then, that Rand Jr. was the author of the Mohawk Valley Strategy: he knew how to orchestrate a 20th-century *business* — in running it as a ruthless overseer of industrial output, and in administering it as a savvy accountant.

Business was also in Rand Jr.’s blood. The Remington Rand Corporation was born out of a merger between his father’s office supplies business, from which he had been fired due to his zealous ambition, and his own, more innovative company, which he had built after being jettisoned from his father’s. While the Senior Rand had parlayed his earlier career in banking into a lucrative small business, Rand Jr. represented the apotheosis of this vision, the modern businessman *after* the monopolistic barons of the Gilded Age. In 1926, he wrote a book detailing his learnings at the helm of a large corporation: *Assuring Business Profits, or How to Run Any Business on a Big Business Basis.*

A decade later, Rand Jr. penned the Mohawk Valley Strategy as a confident re-assertion of the strength of the businessman, defining himself in opposition to labor when most of the nation blamed the Depression on big business. If anything, he was early to the game.

*James Rand Jr. (left) alongside Harvey Firestone, the tire magnate whose own dealings with labor include the establishment of a rubber plantation in Liberia in the 1920s that was widely known to forcibly employ children. Credit: Getty Images*

Another decade later, in a time of growing national bounty and a place about an hour East of Remington Rand, Lemuel Boulware was articulating his own business playbook in Schenectady’s General Electric (GE) plants. Earlier, GE had been known as a worker’s company, a progressive shop that actively repudiated the legacies of the 19th-century robber barons, inspiring a vision of the big company as a small family. Throughout the 1930s and early 1940s, GE leadership distanced itself from the vigorous, pro-business zeal of companies like Remington Rand; in the same year as the Remington Rand strike, GE signed a nationwide contract with the United Electrical Workers (UE) without a strike.

But after an enormous national strike in 1946 — one of the largest in the nation’s history that included UE locals at GE — GE leadership became convinced that it could not co-exist with its unions. They brought in Boulware, a marketing and PR expert originally from Kentucky, to slay the beast of organized labor. Upon arriving at GE, he first promulgated a carefully curated PR strategy that touted GE’s “leadership in all heart-warming local activities.” But such quaint notions of corporate welfarism were quickly dispensed in favor of a more hardball tactic.

Boulware’s key practical innovation, which would come to be called “Boulwarism,” was the “take it or leave it” offer. Under Boulwarism, GE leadership would listen to union demands, produce a rigorously assessed and thoroughly detailed counteroffer, and refuse to negotiate further. Though this was itself a novel strategy, it was only enabled by GE’s increasingly vocal threats to move to the South or abroad, where labor was cheap and unorganized. Boulware translated the shifting geographic and racial distribution of American labor into a simple mathematical problem comprehensible to GE’s workers: where would you put your money if you knew that costs were rising and profits were falling?

*(Boulwarism was also facilitated by the passage of the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947, which significantly curbed union power. Its congressional sponsor, Ohio Senator Robert Taft, a.k.a. “Mr. Republican,” was the son of President William Howard Taft and is perhaps the single individual most responsible for the decline of labor power in America. Like the Rand family, the Taft family arrived in Massachusetts decades before the American Revolution and has a long history in business.*

*The Tafts became slowly diffused into the broader coalition of American business interests by the end of the 20th century. Their declining power was best symbolized by the Cleveland mayoral election of 1967, when Seth Taft, Robert’s nephew, lost to the eventual first Black mayor of a major city in the US, Carl Stokes.)*

But perhaps the most consequential change ushered in by Boulware was the least tangible. During his time at GE, Boulware oversaw a top-to-bottom internal re-education campaign, during which managers were trained to read conservative publications like the *National Review* and free market economists like Friedrich Hayek, and directed to treat every subsequent interaction with workers as a seminar in business principles. As historian Kim Phillips-Fein writes in *Invisible Hands*, “few… corporations during the 1950s fought unions with the same publicity and intensity as GE, and few developed such a highly politicized internal corporate culture.” One GE spokesperson who most thoroughly imbibed the lessons of Boulware was a former actor who wistfully remembered his time at GE as his crash-course in political economy. His name was Ronald Reagan.

*Reagan on the floor of a GE factory in Schenectady in the 1950s.*

As much I’m inclined to discuss Reagan’s time at GE, that is not a matter for today. I seek to impress upon you both the lineage of American political ideology and its geographic origins. The Remington Rand strike was a primal scene for 20th century America — set, of course, in the primal American setting of New York State — in which capital and labor clashed in a fledgling war. Large-scale, federated unions were a creature of the New Deal, and the corporate response to such coordinated union action had not yet been coherently articulated. When corporations placed their coordinated thumbs on the scale, their practices provided the intellectual fodder for conservatism in America: from Andrew Carnegie’s Pinkertons to James Rand Jr.’s Mohawk Valley Formula, from Rand Jr. to Boulwarism, and from Boulware through a murkier path, becoming inflected with Barry Goldwater’s small-business revolution, Richard Nixon’s courting of blue-collar ethnic whites, Jerry Falwell’s Christian Right, Phyllis Schlafly’s politics of cultural grievance, and so on and so forth until we arrived at the present moment.

The decline of most traditional urban American centers through the latter half of the 20th century — which has, ironically, positioned them for rapid, land rent-maxxing growth in the 21st — is part of the same history of this conservative tradition. We must understand the intellectual origins of these ideas to see these places in full.

*(P.S. I find it very funny that Ronald Reagan, the prince of American conservatism — at least in the popular consciousness — was initially a New Dealer. At one point in his career, he was the seven-term leader of the Screen Actor’s Guild (SAG), winning raises and residual payments for actors. Then, for GE, he was a union buster, a frat boy deployed to the factory floor and the manager’s office to slap backs and grease palms. Ever the empty vessel, he was by his final term in office a husk of a human being, puppeteered by the Christian Right, the military-industrial complex, and the chambers of commerce of a few Sun Belt cities. This titanic figure in American political history was just a stooge bending over backwards to comport with the mores of contemporary politicking.)*

***Mystery and Intrigue***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F9cababc2-a55a-421d-ad8c-c8db09a91981_2304x1536.jpeg)

*Utica’s Union Station. Like all of these Upstate cities and towns, it was once the engine of the local economy and was adorned as such.*

In the present moment, I find myself in Utica, New York. It’s the evening of November 19th, 2025, and the temperature is dropping rapidly. My plan was to spend the afternoon in Utica, drive an hour over to Syracuse, stay the night, and then dip out to the Finger Lakes. But I spent too long at the locks of the Erie Canal near Ilion, NY, where Remington Rand’s factory workers went on strike nearly 100 years ago, and now I’ve pulled up to Utica’s resplendent Union Station with just an hour of daylight left. Supposing that if there is a place to skimp on this trip, it’s this flyover city of 65,000, I’m content to do a loop up and down main street and go.

I use the loo at Union Station and am stunned by its milky marble columns and orange glow. There are three police officers here, noticeably large men, and the few trains that entered the station today have long since departed. It’s dead-quiet other than the sounds of police banter echoing off the cold stone. I’m feeling intrigued, as I have all trip, but now there’s something fresh in the air, as if I’m in an exotic foreign country: New Yorkistan, Central New York, hours away from the fuss and familiarity of the Hudson Valley.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdbe20afd-de62-4c59-9cd8-f199b89d15cd_2304x1536.jpeg)

*The view from Roscoe Conkling Park, Utica. Roscoe was your favorite politician’s favorite politician, at least when politicians could read.*

I pull out of Union Station and route to Genesee Street, Utica’s main street. On the way, I pass several fabulous buildings on Broad Street, each with a little nametag sliced into stone. *Doyle, Rathbun, Webb*: Irish and English names on buildings erected when names were Irish and English and not American.

“Genesee” is a common name for places upstate, where it’s most closely associated with the Genesee River, which spun the flour mills of Rochester with its turbulent waters before depositing silt and farm runoff into Lake Ontario. Farther upriver is a continuous string of bucolic towns, each given life by the minerals of the highlands of Northern Pennsylvania.

Utica is nowhere to be found along the river’s course, but Genesee is an apt name for its main street. It’s believed to come from the Seneca word “Gen-nis-hee-yo,” meaning pleasant or beautiful valley. One can only imagine what Genesee Street looked like in the 1950s and 1960s, when it housed rows of stately elm trees that were ultimately wiped out by Dutch Elm Disease.

The Northern boundary of Utica is a flat East-to-West line along the Mohawk River, making Utica almost a rectangle pressed up against water on one side and hills on the other. Genesee Street cuts diagonally across the rectangle, beginning where the Mohawk River meets one of those great beacons of postindustrial capitalism, a Wendy’s, and traveling uphill to the Southwestern edge of Utica, becoming increasingly historically rich as it gains altitude.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff6a95e3d-ae41-4fcf-aff2-16cc1a4300d9_2304x1536.jpeg)

*The St. Francis de Sales School, closed in the 1990s, Utica.*

Creeping slowly up the street in the right lane, I’m taken aback by the level of pedestrian activity in this ramshackle downtown. There are people milling about: one fellow just walked out of “Antiques and Such,” and another’s just exited the Adirondack Bank Building, one of the few grand buildings in this section of downtown that appears to be open. I realize I’m closer to the Adirondacks, that great, remote mound of earth in Upstate New York — commonly mistaken as a continuation of the Appalachians rather than the uplift of some of the most ancient bedrock on earth — than home.

There’s something uniquely haphazard about this street and the energy it’s emanating. The Adirondack Bank Building has some art deco upward-ness to it, as well as at least three sections of Romanesque pillars scattered across the building’s façade. They feel like arbitrary declarations of human beauty amid 15 stories of vertical bricks.

Now I’m bombing up Genessee Street and I’m feeling giddy. There’s a palatial art deco theater on the left and a dismal mansion on the right. A Bosnian club is up around the corner, and a bubbling artisan coffee roaster is just past the next intersection. The buildings are smaller and smaller as I drive up the hill, but they continue to represent any and all manner of thing: an ochre Victorian house, the gleaming golden dome of an Orthodox Church, and a few gas stations thrown in for good measure.

Nearing the horizon, where my ascent would seem to end, I pull over to check my location on Google Maps. In my rearview mirror, there’s a phenomenal scene brewing. The fading sun offers a final fuschia, soft, gentle, and golden on the barren trees of the surrounding foothills. An unadorned, stucco mosque poses thoughtfully in the valley; by the time I decide to call it on Genessee it’s disappeared into the night.

What’s going on here?! I have to find out, so I cancel my room in Syracuse, lose 50 bucks, and find the closest hotel in Utica I can get with some kind of points.

***New Lebanon***

What had I seen earlier in that day that had excited me so? Sure, there was wonderful architecture on Genesee, but I’d already seen a lot of cool shapes and ornaments on the trip. I considered some of the institutions I’d seen, which included a mosque, a Bosnian social club, and an Orthodox Church — markers of difference, of identity, but also of belonging. I greatly enjoyed seeing these immigrant groups proudly making their marks on main street, pressed in between some of old America’s architectural and industrial treasures. There were layered narratives emerging, immediately visible to the eye but not readily comprehensible; stories of industry and immigration, death and revival. I soon found out that my hotel, located 5-10 minutes outside of Utica-proper in a suburban strip mall, had brought me closer to a universally acclaimed symbol of this history: Greek yogurt.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7b219ef4-ea6d-4b64-b184-7a978f21462b_2318x1536.jpeg)

*Uptown Theatre, Genesee Street, Utica.*

Hamdi Ulukaya, the Turkish immigrant founder of Chobani, started making Chobani yogurt at a decrepit Kraft plant about 40 minutes South of Utica. Ulukaya, who comes from a family of sheep herders and cheesemakers, purchased the Kraft plant against the warnings of his lawyer and accountant, who both threatened to drop him if he followed through. In the 20 years since its founding, Chobani has become a staple in refrigerators across the country (or in coastal fridges, at least), and Ulukaya has become a figure of local renown and national prominence.

In 2016, Ulukaya first reported that he had arranged regular transportation from Utica to the Chobani factory, where employees from at least 19 countries would be greeted by translators and interpreters skilled in 16 languages. He does this because he is intimately familiar with their stories, himself an ethnic Kurd from an Eastern Anatolian nomadic group, now producing dairy products in the Mohawk Valley. He also does it simply because he is able: Ulukaya is now one of the 300 richest men in the world, and the generous profit-sharing model he’s developed at Chobani is unlikely to affect his personal well-being.

His altruism has generated relentless smears from across the right-wing and even some mainstream media. Upon review, Ulukaya’s only sins seem to be that he’s enjoyed unqualified success, and that he’s employed countless immigrants and refugees in his factories, whom he’s compensated fairly, even handsomely.

This is the story of Utica, a city built, weathered, and resurrected by immigrants. Workers at Chobani are from Iraq, Somalia, and Afghanistan, among other nations. They are, each of them, a plus-one to a city population that is growing for the first time in decades. In the 1950s, during Utica’s “Loom to Boom” period, when its time as the “Knit Goods Capital of the World” gave way to wartime and post-war defense manufacturing, there were over 100,000 people in the city. By the turn of the century, there were less than 60,000, an astonishing loss of nearly half the city’s residents, akin to more notorious population bombs like those that hit Detroit or Glasgow.

*Boarded-up Victorian homes, Utica.*

To piece together this cosmically vast story of people from cities and jungles and deserts who now find themselves in remote upstate New York, I turned to a number of sources. I was initially shocked at how much has been written, documented, and narrated about Utica. That shock turned to awe as I grew to feel the bone-deep pride Uticans have for their adopted hometown, which has arisen at least partly because the stories of Uticans are so rich.

In my first few Google searches, I kept encountering a name, Zogby, that piqued my curiosity. It was paired with unremarkable first names like John and James, so initially I punted my thoughts and kept reading. But there it was in an article about a 1980’s mayoral election, and there it was again in the name of a local insurance broker: Zogby, Zogby, Zogby.

*Here is a fun fact:* in the aftermath of the utopian presidential election of 1996, only one pollster could claim that they had accurately predicted the election’s results. Where most polling groups had foretold of a double-digit, blowout Bill Clinton win over inveterate goofball Bob Dole, John Zogby Strategies correctly determined that Clinton would win by ~8 points. Because polling, as a discipline, had not yet been tarred, feathered, and entirely discredited, John Zogby was touted as a genius. “All hail Zogby, the Maverick predictor,” wrote The Washington Post that year: he had “conquered the 1996 election.” By the turn of the century, when his polls again closely tracked the outcomes of the 2000 presidential election, he had become the insider pollster for Washington politicos.

For Zogby, this was nothing new. He had been the only pollster to predict George Pataki’s win over incumbent Mario Cuomo in the 1994 New York gubernatorial election. 13 years earlier, back in Utica, Zogby had experienced his first, bittersweet triumph, positing that an outsider candidate in the 1981 Utica mayoral race would receive 14-15% of the vote. That candidate, Mr. Zogby himself, received exactly 15% of the vote, which slammed the door shut on his political career and threw open a new one in the world of civic soothsaying.

Zogby, now 77, attributes his persistent predictive proclivity to his lifelong decision to stay in Utica. If the folks around him aren’t talking about a political issue, he says, it doesn’t have salience. The incredible demographic cross-section of Utica residents, which spans races, ages, nationalities, and even political inclinations, has offered him a consistent sounding board throughout his illustrious career. It’s also helped him develop some of his more controversial polling tools, like weighting polling results based on sampled demographics. Many in the highly scientific world of polling regard this as indefensible manipulation and mashing of numbers; to the national media, Zogby’s methods have been a sign of his preternatural gift.

John Zogby is the son of a Lebanese immigrant to Utica. His father put him and his brother — equally famed in niche political circles as the founder of the Arab American Institute — through school with the meager profits he made from his Utica grocery store. His mother, who was raised in Pennsylvania by two Lebanese immigrants, was for decades a stalwart teacher in Utica’s declining public schools. Growing up, his friends’ fathers, cousins, and uncles worked at GE (in open war with its unions, as discussed), or the successor to Remington Rand, Sperry Rand’s UNISYS (forever antagonistic to its workers, as discussed), or even at Griffiss Air Base in nearby Rome, NY (which would close in 1995 after years of downsizing). Uticans, long subject to inexorable, unpredictable economic forces, have watched proudly as one of their native sons has built a career on telling the future. In media appearances dating back to 2004, from outlets like The Daily Show and Fortune magazine, Zogby speaks of Utica with a deep, abiding love, and the measured tone of someone who lived through the depths of the city’s despair.

For every Zogby — quiet, resolute, numerically literate — there is a Mayor Ed Hanna: anarchical.

Now, Lebanon is a country very far away from New York with a relatively small footprint in the contemporary US. But this was not always the case. Between 1880 and 1924, nearly 250,000 Syrians and Lebanese arrived in the US (their *national* identities not yet established), most of them Christian men fleeing Ottoman military conscription. *(These immigrants were the foundation for Lower Manhattan’s “Little Syria,” which, through successive waves of city-sanctioned urban destruction and natural out-migration has ceased to exist entirely.)* This first wave of Lebanese immigration to the US consisted largely of men who were, by trade, traders. They developed vast peddling networks across the Eastern US, selling shaving blades, lace, and thread, which helps explain their premature appearance in the fossil records of places like Fort Wayne, IN, and Utica. John Zogby’s father likely immigrated as part of this singular Levantine wave, though Zogby describes him as an illegal immigrant upon arrival, which suggests that he disembarked his boat after 1924, when the US effectively outlawed most international immigration.

Edward “Ed” Arnold Hanna was born in 1922, about 50 years before John Zogby, but, similarly, to Lebanese parents who had settled in Utica. Like Zogby’s family, Hanna’s assimilated relatively cleanly into the physical and social fabric of Utica’s East Side Italians as entrepreneurial, educated Christians. Hanna’s parents, who are listed as Anthony and Sophie Hanna, almost certainly anglicized their names upon arriving in the US. This is where the comparisons between Ed and John end, and where we may unveil the portrait of Ed Hanna as The Municipal Joker.

One of the wonderful minor delights of reading about small cities and towns is that there is often just one *keeper of the keys*: one person who sits in their attic all day poring over local newspaper clippings, birth records, and political speeches and synthesizing them into a Wordpress blog. Utica’s *keeper of the keys* is Wikipedia editor “Apocheir,” who, over the course of five months in 2023, wrote for Ed Hanna a balanced, thorough Wikipedia entry, one that might be expected of a national-level politician or big-city mayor. Apocheir’s altruistic contributions to Utica history, sourced from dozens, perhaps even hundreds of Utica Observer-Dispatch records, are pretty much the only comprehensive account of the life and times of Ed Hanna.

Before turning it over to Apocheir, I’ll offer some brief biographical notes on Ed Hanna. Hanna first became incredibly, ambiguously wealthy as a consultant for Rochester-based Eastman Kodak. Once he solidified his personal fortune, he became a guy who spent entire city council hearings standing on two feet and heckling. Soon, he simply could not stop running for office — Zogby’s 1981 run occurred during the only decade between 1950 and 2000 that did not feature the louder-than-life Hanna in regional politics.

In 1956, Hanna was appointed to the Central New York State Parks Commission and immediately began raising Hell. It was in the Parks system that he made his name politically by tangling with Robert Moses, whom he demanded be dismissed as chairman of the State Council of Parks. For most politicians, opposing Moses amounted to a forced career change, perhaps into sales or insurance. But for Hanna, it was an introduction to a career that would become defined by pointing fingers at the men that ran New York’s political machines. In 1974, after a stint in the State assembly, Hanna won Utica’s mayoral election on a campaign that cautiously distanced himself from Rufus Elefante, the belligerent head of Utica’s Italian Democratic political machine.

After four tumultuous years in office that coincided with tremendous job losses across Central New York, Hanna disappeared for some time, then popped back up in Paris (France, not Texas). He returned to Utica in 1996 as local papers were discovering that city government could indeed fall farther into debt and disgrace, and he won that year’s mayoral election, over 20 years after his first term in office. He resigned and left Utica in 2000 amid whispers of his sexual harassment of male city employees. Then, drawn seemingly by a magnet, he returned to Utica in 2007, when he again ran for mayor. He lost, then passed away two years later in 2009.

*Abandoned signage business, flanked by the freeway, Utica.*

To me, Hanna’s political ideas — his ideology, if you could even call it that — were oriented around nebulous ideas of trust and fairness. In practice, this amounted to regular budget cuts, slashing of the city payroll, and, perhaps more positively, a purging of the city’s residual machine politics. We must sift through his theatricality to see those ideas manifest in policy, so here are examples of both from Apocheir’s Wikipedia page:

*“[His] opponents accused him of egomania, instability, making misleading statements about budget matters, a lack of foresight and long-term plans, and an unwillingness to either listen or delegate.”*

*“In his first year, Hanna cut 225 positions and fired 15 city executives: he took on the management of five city departments himself.”*

*“One of Hanna’s first acts was to order city policemen to salute citizens on the street, which they refused.”*

*“He fired the entire Public Works department, then rehired them the next day.”*

*“Hanna was reported to work more than 18-hour days, consuming mainly saltine crackers and milk.”*

*“A former editor of the Utica newspapers joined Hanna's administration… but quit after two months: his letter of resignation criticized Hanna's autocratic and abusive governing style and expressed fears that city government could collapse badly enough that the state would need to step in.”*

A couple hundred miles away, 20 years later, I’m clacking vigorously on my keyboard in New York City, and it’s easy for me to condemn Hanna’s brand of demagoguery-cum-austerity. But many in the city appreciate Hanna for ushering in the post-machine era of Utica politics, one with diminished state capacity but less flagrant political corruption. Whatever his enduring legacy may be in Utica, it is clear that he touched the lives of many ordinary Uticans, who will maintain a level of reverence for him as long as they’re alive. Here are some more personal notes from folks in Utica, posted on his public obituary at legacy.com:

*“Dear Mayor Hanna....in May of 1974 my Father marched me ( I was 18) into your office to ask for a summer job.....I was scared to death until I saw you behind your desk in your office eating oreo cookies and milk. You heard my plea and gave me a job. You were a great inspiration to me and I will never forget you...ever.”*

*“Ed, You were a maverick, but that’s what this city needed. There will never be another like you.Best wishes to the Hanna family, God Bless you”*

*“In 1976 we were a struggling young married couple with a child and my husband needed a job. I went to see Mayor Hanna. He gave me a letter stating when he was released from the Army he would have a job waiting for him. The day came when my husband showed up at City Hall, letter in hand, and Mayor Hanna hired him for the Parks Department. Later he transfered to the DPW where he worked for 5 years. Mayor Hanna was a unique individual who was loved by many. He made a personal impact to our lives and we will always remember him for that and the many wonderful things he did for the City of Utica.”*

My favorite Ed Hanna Moment (TM): some time in the late 1990s, [CBS came to Utica to interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a9ftEoFOexI) its riotous mayor. I *urge* you to watch the segment. It begins with incredulous CBS reporter Bernard Goldberg saying of his interviewee: *“He says he has the worst job in town, maybe in the country. He once called Utica a terrible place to live. Did I mention that he’s the mayor?”* Then there are shots of Hanna running around abandoned lots, yelling with no discernible purpose. Then, as he opens his ceaselessly twitching mouth, he extolls the virtues of his mayoralty: *“All the bullshit, all the… uhh… horse shit, and all the other shit, is over with.”*

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8adb8fed-41a4-418c-8e0d-0e2c801e3d43_2318x1537.jpeg)

*The former Jeannelle’s Disha Vu diner, Utica.*

To conclude this chapter, we must return from the personal to the ideological contributions of Lebanese men, this time regarding American conservatism. Big business conservatism, in its manifestation in political economy, is plainly responsible for pain in cities across the country like Utica. *(That’s **not** to identify the Republican party as the sole source of malaise; the moderation of the Democratic party throughout the late 20th century, especially in its estrangement from labor and its pursuit of global capital, is equally at fault.)* Behind the scenes, one of the architects of mid-century business conservatism was William J. Baroody Sr., the son of Lebanese immigrants from Massachusetts. Baroody was a principal advisor to Barry Goldwater’s 1964 presidential campaign, and his son, William Baroody Jr., was a close confidante of several Republican presidents including Richard Nixon and Ronald Reagan. The Baroody clan were one of the chief shepherds of conservative ideology throughout the 20th century, effectively translating the free market conservatism of New Deal backlash into a national coalition of reactionaries of any and all provenance. Lesser known than some of the aforementioned conservative titans, the Baroodys have quietly been one of the most influential families in the history of American politics.

Immigrants all carry within them a desire for assimilation and belonging in the promised land, which competes with a fierce sense of personal responsibility in a scarce, depleted world. Either of those inclinations may be channeled into a politics of solidarity, or an ethos of individualism. Which way will you go, Lebanese man? Will our future leaders be Zogbys, Hannas, or Baroodys?

***Fire and Ice***

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F17ac695c-fc53-4747-b5a7-c1dfc42d98bf_2318x1537.jpeg)

*Here is a good place to stop if your brakes give out on you.*

It would be a disservice to Hanna — and to Utica — not to describe some of the conditions the city found itself in by the time of his second term. To do so, we must embark on a brief trip downstate back to New York City.

In 1967, a rogue camera from a Yankees vs. Orioles game at Yankee Stadium floated a few hundred feet above home plate to show what lay behind the stadium grounds. A rowhouse was ablaze some three hundred feet north of the warning track, raw flames licking high into the air, so close to the game that you could almost smell the burning wood and stone through the camera. “Ladies and gentlemen, the Bronx is burning,” declared the stoic play-by-play announcer.

By the end of the following decade, the Bronx, home to nearly a million and a half people, had burned. One in five of its homes were gone. The South Bronx, in particular, had become synonymous with urban decline and had hosted a bevy of concerned politicians, like Jimmy Carter, who trod upon its rubble in highly sensationalized displays of compassion.

The reasons for the Decade of Fire were multiple and complex, or maybe they were all just the same thing. For a brief period in the 1940s and early 50s before the calamities of deindustrialization and white flight, sections of the Bronx were some of the most integrated places in the country, little utopias where Italian children could play alongside their Jewish classmates, or their Black teammates, or their Puerto Rican neighbors.

Then throughout the 1950s and 1960s, when the wave of Black and Puerto Rican families arriving in NYC began to crest, the demographic scale tilted (especially towards Puerto Ricans from the agrarian countryside, who were fleeing on the heels of forced national industrialization via Operation Bootstrap). The city embarked on a scorched-earth program of urban renewal, which razed Black and Puerto Rican neighborhoods in Manhattan and left their former residents scrambling for new housing*. A few remained in Manhattan, mostly North of Central Park in the enormous public housing developments that were springing up daily. Many moved to the Bronx, where similar housing developments grew to dominate the landscape, first built through direct government subsidy, and then through the all-consuming public private partnership. Some brave families marched outwards to New Jersey, or Westchester County, or Long Island, where they were often denied housing or intimidated back into the city by bombs and vile letters.

**(I am deeply opposed to both the theory and practice of midcentury urban renewal, but I feel obligated to note that in some cases, truly dilapidated neighborhoods were refashioned into livable areas accessible to a few of the recently evicted families.)*

The Bronx was always poor, but by the 1960s its poverty became different in kind, not just in magnitude: it became a locus for expropriation and not just systemic indifference. White Bronxites — anybody with the means, really — fled for the suburbs, where refugees of urban decline who were newly declared to be white could indeed find asylum. All of a sudden there were honest-to-goodness Italians and Jews and Irishmen in single-family homes on Long Island, and where there were once miles and miles of hardscrabble melting pot locales in the Bronx there were now isolated pockets of whiteness in a sea of newly-identified minorities.

Thousands of apartments in the Bronx were left vacant and rotting, and thousands of other apartments in the Bronx were crowded with 10 or more residents in a single unit. Bleeding jobs and borrowing aggressively in the short-term, the city was wholly unprepared to put its finger on the scale. As the city’s finances turned increasingly sour, it hired independent consultants to recommend budget cuts, including RAND Corporation, a pioneering data analytical research firm that rapidly expanded past its origins in the military-industrial complex into the domain of municipal finance. RAND analyzed the city’s public expenditure and identified a slew of firehouse cuts that could save the city a substantial amount in operating costs. Nearly all of these cuts were in poor, predominantly Black and Puerto Rican neighborhoods that were already experiencing elevated rates of arson. While the city pursued those closures, recent reviews have shown that it actually opened new firehouses at the rate of the Bronx cuts, mostly in wealthy neighborhoods in Manhattan.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F815ea0c9-1df7-4a45-8d97-fff692b24ebc_2304x1536.jpeg)

*Puerto Rican flag hanging from a window, Utica.*

So when landlords started hiring local teens and young adults — nearly all unemployed — to burn their own buildings down and cash out on insurance claims, they had at their disposal a small army of willing fire-starters. The city’s reactionary apparatus, already in the process of decamping it, blamed the fires on Black and Puerto Rican immigrants who they felt simply couldn’t be trusted to live in the city. The fires affirmed the racist beliefs of many city politicians, like a spokesperson for the Municipal Assistance Corporation who told a journalist that the underlying reasons for New York’s fiscal problems were [“the fucking blacks and Puerto Ricans.”](https://www.versobooks.com/products/1419-the-assassination-of-new-york?srsltid=AfmBOoqFDXX9Yz4MYsBSW2K0LxhCDwMMNh8_qKhBtkmZU-cYCsfA6c3w)

Today, we know that the massive delta between real property values — declining by the second and increasingly costly to maintain — and assessed insurance values drove much of the burning. That process may in fact have been kickstarted by the Federal Government, which, in 1968, agreed to reinsure property insurance in redlined neighborhoods as insurers moved out of urban cores after the 1967 riots. One scandalous case, emblematic of the emerging circuitry of global finance, centered on a scheme by a rogue Lloyd’s of London partner to back risky policies in the Bronx and purchase reinsurance on those policies, which Brazilian banks were bundling and re-selling as high-yield financial instruments.

Thus, the Bronx burned. But it was not just the Bronx but in fact nearly every urban area in America. These same dynamics — tax revenue collapse driven by rapid, large-scale demographic change (and subsequent racism), private greed, state shrinkage, and misaligned market incentives — played out similarly in cities of all sizes. It took about 20 years, but the same gap between market and insurance valuations finally caught up with Utica.

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F493d495b-e050-4086-b8c8-9ffd875daf45_2318x1536.jpeg)

*Probably once-bustling, now abandoned seafood restaurant, Utica.*

It started with a few fires in the late 1980s, mostly in shuttered warehouses, but it quickly became a pandemic. In Utica, there are fewer neighborhoods and fewer hard boundaries between them, like the cliffs and forests of the Bronx. A home may be ablaze in a neighborhood across the city from yours. But you will see the smoke, smell the transfiguration of vinyl siding — ethylene and chlorine, really — into air. Then you might want to leave your home. Then you get a situation in which one burned home makes it ever-so-slightly more likely that another home on the block burns or is abandoned altogether. Then that probability doubles, and triples, until the entire block is burned, and firefighters and arson cops are too busy contending with the same thing two blocks over to do anything about it.

By the end of the 1990s, after several years with more than 300 fires each, Utica’s physical fabric had literally been consumed by the economic logic of decline. Now we can begin to understand what Ed Hanna meant when he said he had the worst job in the world. The fires had stopped by the 2010s, but they had been fast, intense, and psychologically shattering. Reconstruction of properties destroyed by arson is slow, plodding, and subject to such inanities as the Applicable Federal Rate. It is both macroeconomic and microeconomic, as much a process born from mayoral policy initiative as collective spirit. Ed didn’t have it easy.

It’s worth mentioning that this sort of grotesque history is uniquely American. We may gesture vaguely to the post-industrial decline of regions like the West Midlands in the UK, where Welsh and other rural migrants uncovered the coal that birthed the Industrial Revolution. But no matter how bad things got, there is simply no global analogue for the sheer emptiness and devastation that scars much of America’s old cities. Toxteth, a historically working-class neighborhood of Liverpool, famously burned in the 1980s due in part to political agitation and protest, but more so due to structural economic factors (that is to say, economic death). Despite the fires and a globally renowned program of austerity, Toxteth never fell into ruin like so much of Central New York. This may be because much of Utica’s housing stock was wood-frame while the streets of Toxteth are mostly packed with brick structures. It may be because America has some inscrutable pathology of racial violence that channels economics into disaster and disaster into violence against Black communities (indeed, the worst-hit neighborhood in Utica, Cornhill, had become its largest and perhaps only Black community by the 1990s). It is certainly because around 40% of the UK’s population lived in council (public) housing in the 1980s, which effectively eliminated the possibility of landlord-induced arson.

Whatever the reason may be, much of urban America was returned to a vegetative state by the turn of the century, with no global analogue for redevelopment to learn from. This should have been the death knell for Utica, which in the early 2000s slumped to its lowest population since 1900.

But if you recall from my effusive praise of Chobani Yogurt above, Utica survived. It is not a city in ruin. Its Little Italy, the old East Side, is abuzz with activity. Roscoe Conkling Park, sitting high above the city in its Southeast corner, remains immaculately maintained and a wonderful spot for a sunrise hike. Houses, once burned, withered into vacant lots which one day were purchased by Bosnian refugees, who, with a little bit of assistance from the Mohawk Valley Resource Center for Refugees, dreamed of opening traditional Bosnian Bakeries in the remote Arctic that is Central New York. They dreamed of rebuilding their lives wherever they could, and it turns out that Utica is actually a place where you can live out your dream, if you’re willing to put up with the Ed Hanna types and the occasional rabid deer rampaging down Genesee Street.

The story of modern Utica, of the complex mesh of American empire through which yogurt is strained and delivered to Kroger, must wait for the next part in this series. There are Italians to discuss, the most per capita of any city in New York, in fact, along with Iraqis, Karens, Somalians, and of course Dominicans and Puerto Ricans, lest we forget we are in New York. I hope to have set the stage for that discussion here through several seemingly unrelated diatribes on American conservatism, labor history, Lebanese men, and fire. I pray it all comes together in Part 2 of Part 3 (Utica). A man can dream.
